# RAG Explained Without the Buzzwords

Large Language Models (LLMs) can answer questions, summarize information, write content, and perform many other tasks. But there is one important limitation:

**An LLM does not automatically have access to every piece of information you want it to use.**

What if you want an AI assistant to answer questions about your company's internal documents, product manuals, customer policies, or a private knowledge base?

This is where **RAG** comes in.

## What is RAG?

**RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation.**

It is a technique that allows an AI application to **retrieve relevant information from external sources and provide that information to an LLM as context before generating an answer.**

In simple terms:

> **RAG helps an AI find the right information before it answers.**

Instead of expecting the model to know everything, we give it access to the information it needs.

## Why Do We Need RAG?

Imagine a company has thousands of documents containing:

* Product documentation
* Internal policies
* Customer support guides
* Pricing information
* Technical documentation
* Company procedures

Now an employee asks:

> "What is our refund policy for annual subscriptions?"

A general-purpose LLM may not know the company's private refund policy.

We could try to put every document into the prompt, but that would be inefficient and impractical.

Instead, a RAG system can search the company's knowledge base, find the relevant section about annual subscription refunds, and provide that information to the LLM.

The model can then generate an answer based on the retrieved information.

## How Does RAG Work?

A basic RAG system follows a simple process:

**User Question**

↓

**Search for Relevant Information**

↓

**Retrieve Relevant Content**

↓

**Give Content to the LLM**

↓

**Generate Answer**

For example:

> **Question:** "How long do customers have to request a refund?"

The system searches the available documents and finds:

> "Customers may request a refund within 30 days of their annual subscription purchase."

That information is provided to the LLM as context.

The LLM can then respond:

> "Customers can request a refund within 30 days of purchasing an annual subscription."

The important part is that the answer was generated using **retrieved information**.

## What Does "Retrieval-Augmented Generation" Mean?

The name sounds complicated, but it can be broken down into three simple words.

### Retrieval

The system **retrieves** relevant information from an external source.

### Augmented

That information is **added to the context** provided to the AI model.

### Generation

The LLM uses the available context to **generate the final response**.

So:

**Retrieve → Add Context → Generate**

That's RAG.

## Where Does the Information Come From?

The information used by a RAG system can come from many different sources.

For example:

* PDFs
* Websites
* Company documentation
* Databases
* Knowledge bases
* Text files
* Product manuals
* Customer support articles

The important thing is that the AI application has a way to search and retrieve relevant information from those sources.

## What are Embeddings?

This is where another important AI concept appears: **embeddings**.

An embedding is a numerical representation of information that captures aspects of its meaning.

For example, consider these two questions:

> "How do I reset my password?"

and:

> "I forgot my password. How can I change it?"

They use different words, but their meanings are very similar.

An embedding model can convert these pieces of text into numerical vectors that represent their semantic meaning.

This makes it possible to compare pieces of information based on meaning rather than simply matching the exact words.

## What is a Vector Database?

Once documents are converted into embeddings, those vectors can be stored in a **vector database**.

A vector database is designed to store and search these numerical representations efficiently.

A simplified process looks like:

**Documents**

↓

**Split into smaller sections**

↓

**Create embeddings**

↓

**Store embeddings**

↓

**User asks a question**

↓

**Create an embedding for the question**

↓

**Search for similar vectors**

↓

**Retrieve relevant sections**

↓

**Send them to the LLM**

↓

**Generate the answer**

This retrieval process is one of the core components of many RAG applications.

## Why are Documents Split into Smaller Sections?

Large documents are usually divided into smaller pieces, often called **chunks**.

Imagine a 100-page employee handbook.

If a user asks:

> "How many vacation days do employees receive?"

The system doesn't need to send the entire 100-page document to the LLM.

Instead, the document can be divided into smaller sections.

The retrieval system can then find the specific section discussing vacation days and provide that relevant content to the model.

Good **chunking** is important because chunks that are too large may contain unnecessary information, while chunks that are too small may lose important context.

## RAG vs Traditional Search

Traditional keyword search might look for exact words.

For example, searching for:

> "refund policy"

might prioritize documents containing those exact words.

RAG systems can use **semantic search**, which focuses more on meaning.

A user could ask:

> "Can I get my money back if I cancel my subscription?"

Even if the document uses the phrase "subscription refund policy," semantic search can still identify it as relevant.

This makes RAG particularly useful for natural-language questions.

## RAG vs Fine-Tuning

RAG and fine-tuning are often confused, but they solve different problems.

### RAG

RAG gives the model **external information at the time of the request**.

For example:

> "Here is our latest company policy. Use it to answer the question."

### Fine-Tuning

Fine-tuning involves **training an existing model further on a specific dataset** to adapt its behavior or capabilities.

A simple way to remember the difference is:

> **RAG gives the AI information. Fine-tuning changes how the AI behaves.**

If the information changes frequently, such as company policies or product documentation, RAG can be particularly useful because the external knowledge source can be updated without retraining the model.

## Can RAG Prevent AI Hallucinations?

RAG can **reduce the risk of unsupported answers**, but it does not guarantee that an AI system will always be correct.

The quality of the final response depends on several factors:

* Quality of the source documents
* Quality of document chunking
* Retrieval accuracy
* Search strategy
* Amount of relevant context
* LLM behavior
* Prompt design

If the retrieval system finds the wrong information, the LLM may still produce an incorrect answer.

This is why RAG systems need evaluation, testing, and monitoring.

## Where is RAG Used?

RAG can be useful anywhere an AI application needs to work with external or specialized information.

Common examples include:

### Customer Support

An AI assistant can retrieve relevant product documentation before answering customer questions.

### Company Knowledge Bases

Employees can ask questions about internal policies, processes, and documentation.

### Documentation Assistants

Developers can ask questions about technical documentation and retrieve relevant sections.

### Research

An AI system can retrieve relevant documents and use them as context for analysis.

### E-commerce

AI assistants can retrieve product information, specifications, and availability.

## A Simple RAG Architecture

A basic RAG application can be visualized as:

```text
                DOCUMENTS
                    ↓
              Split into chunks
                    ↓
                Embeddings
                    ↓
             Vector Database
                    ↓
USER QUESTION → Semantic Search
                    ↓
             Relevant Chunks
                    ↓
             Context + Prompt
                    ↓
                   LLM
                    ↓
                 ANSWER
```

The LLM is only one part of the system.

The retrieval layer provides the information that the model needs to produce a more relevant response.

## The Most Important Idea

RAG isn't a new type of LLM.

It isn't a database.

It isn't fine-tuning.

It is a **system design pattern that connects an AI model to external information.**

The simplest mental model is:

> **LLM = generates the answer**
> **Retrieval = finds the information**
> **RAG = combines both**

## Final Takeaway

RAG allows AI applications to work with information that may not be part of an LLM's original training data.

Instead of asking an AI model to answer everything from what it already knows, a RAG system can:

**Find relevant information → Provide that information as context → Generate an answer**

This makes RAG particularly useful for applications that need to work with **private, specialized, or frequently changing information**.

The concept behind RAG is actually quite simple:

> **Don't make the AI remember everything. Give it a way to find what it needs.**
